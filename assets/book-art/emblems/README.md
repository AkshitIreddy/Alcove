# Alcove binder-tool masters

These 61 SVGs are the complete active book-emblem catalogue. Filenames begin
with the persisted ornament index; the index, reader-facing name and count are
append-only compatibility data. Covers and spines render the same master.

## Drawing contract

- Treat each mark as the impression of one engraved brass finishing tool.
- Use one ink, one broad silhouette and open negative space. Never add a coin,
  badge, plate or enclosing roundel around an emblem.
- Keep the identifying structure in the outside contour. Interior marks are
  support only and may disappear on a 24 px spine.
- Use rounded caps and joins, with roughly 5–7 units of stroke in a 100-unit
  master. The renderer floors the final stroke at 0.9 CSS px.
- Give every name its own silhouette. Similar botanical subjects must differ
  in stance as well as details: a spray travels diagonally, a palmette fans
  upward, a stemmed flower stands vertically, and a knot interlaces.
- Do not add texture, lighting, highlights, shading, blurs or blend modes. The
  book material supplies the ground; the master supplies one flat impression.

## Reference synthesis

The collection research converged on a few useful constraints. Historical
finishing tools are small repeatable impressions whose identity often lets a
binding workshop be recognized. Cover decoration gains hierarchy from a clear
central tool, corner/edge systems and open leather between them. Floral tools,
fleurons, scrolls and heraldic forms are recurrent, while complex pictorial
detail belongs to larger onlays, inlays or panels rather than a tiny spine
stamp. Alcove therefore uses one authored centrepiece per book and preserves
the same tool on its spine.

## Expanded finishing-tool families

Indices 86–117 extend the original set in eight deliberately different
families. Each family changes the subject and the outer silhouette, rather
than recolouring one botanical stamp: formal heraldry (86–89), grand display
marks (90–93), antiquarian symbols (94–97), storybook creatures (98–101),
botanical specimens (102–105), cosy domestic marks (106–109), rustic country
subjects (110–113), and quiet contemplative forms (114–117).
